# Anti-Patterns: Avoid the Generic AI-Generated Look

This reference is a **defensive checklist**, not a visual style guide. Its purpose is to help the Snippetz Labs design skill avoid the familiar combinations that make otherwise polished websites feel templated, synthetic, generic, untrustworthy, or unfinished.

## The governing rule

Do not use the patterns below as defaults, shortcuts, or filler. Before adding a visual treatment, section, claim, animation, or interaction, ask:

1. Does it serve a specific user task or communicate something real about this product?
2. Is it supported by the product's actual brand, content, data, and capabilities?
3. Does it improve comprehension, trust, orientation, or use—not merely make a screenshot look busier or more impressive?
4. Would the interface still feel distinctive if this effect or component were removed?

If a pattern has no product-specific reason, remove it. Prefer a clear, specific, slightly unusual solution over a familiar template assembled from fashionable effects.

**These are not universal bans.** Dark mode, gradients, cards, rounded corners, animation, testimonials, pricing tables, bento layouts, or a conventional hero can all be appropriate. Use them only when the audience, product, content, brand, or tested interaction justifies them. The problem is unearned repetition and formulaic combinations—not the existence of a design technique. Do not force novelty for its own sake.

## 1. Visual clichés and borrowed aesthetics (1–20)

1. Purple-to-blue gradient backgrounds everywhere.
2. Gradient text used on the main headline by default.
3. Neon purple or blue glows behind ordinary UI elements.
4. Frosted-glass cards with excessive backdrop blur.
5. Dark mode as the default for every product, even when it does not suit the audience or context.
6. Beige, cream, and burnt-orange palettes used simply to imitate a current AI-design trend.
7. Every card having exactly the same rounded corners regardless of hierarchy or function.
8. Excessive use of pill-shaped buttons, badges, and labels.
9. Soft shadows on literally everything.
10. A cursor-following glow that serves no useful purpose.
11. Grain or noise texture added to make a plain background feel artificially premium.
12. Random blobs, spheres, and abstract 3D shapes floating in the hero without a connection to the product.
13. A thin colored line across the top or side of every card.
14. Gradient borders around otherwise ordinary containers.
15. Every element receiving a 1px border, whether it needs one or not.
16. The same neutral sans-serif font used throughout every product without considering brand character or editorial hierarchy.
17. Overused trendy font combinations, such as Space Grotesk with Instrument Serif, chosen without a brand reason.
18. One random italic serif word inserted into an otherwise sans-serif headline just to signal sophistication.
19. Overly spaced uppercase text used for every section label.
20. A polished visual style with no recognizable product or brand identity.

## 2. Formulaic landing-page structure (21–40)

21. A centered hero headline with a tiny badge above it on every site.
22. A huge headline split into exactly three lines regardless of the actual copy.
23. A two-column hero with text on the left and a floating dashboard on the right as the automatic default.
24. A fake product screenshot tilted in perspective.
25. Three feature cards placed directly below every hero.
26. Six identical cards arranged in a bento grid simply because bento layouts are fashionable.
27. A feature section with an icon, heading, and paragraph repeated six times.
28. Every section following exactly the same layout and visual rhythm.
29. Alternating left-text/right-image sections throughout the entire page.
30. A giant rounded rectangle containing the entire product preview without a structural reason.
31. A floating notification card layered over a dashboard mockup purely for decoration.
32. A logo strip containing imaginary companies or unsupported customer brands.
33. A testimonial carousel with identical profile pictures, wording, and text lengths.
34. A pricing section with three cards and the middle one highlighted by default, regardless of the actual pricing model.
35. A large gradient CTA banner near the bottom simply to fill the final section.
36. An FAQ section containing the same five predictable questions on every website.
37. A footer with four or five perfectly symmetrical columns when the content does not call for them.
38. Every section separated by enormous amounts of whitespace without regard to reading flow or content density.
39. A long page that feels like a template stretched to fit more sections.
40. A complete absence of an unusual, memorable, product-specific layout or point of view.

## 3. Generic AI marketing copy (41–60)

41. “Unlock the power of AI.”
42. “Supercharge your workflow.”
43. “Transform the way you work.”
44. “Elevate your productivity.”
45. “Your all-in-one platform.”
46. “The future of [industry] is here.”
47. “Revolutionize your experience.”
48. “Seamlessly integrate everything.”
49. “One platform. Endless possibilities.”
50. “Built for the next generation.”
51. Every headline following the “Not X. It's Y.” construction.
52. Excessive use of em dashes.
53. Every paragraph containing three carefully balanced benefits.
54. Repeating the same benefit in the hero, features, CTA, and footer.
55. Excessive use of words such as intelligent, powerful, seamless, effortless, innovative, and cutting-edge.
56. Headlines that sound impressive but do not explain what the product actually does.
57. Feature descriptions that describe only benefits and never explain actual functionality.
58. Fake urgency, such as “Start your transformation today.”
59. Generic CTA buttons such as “Get Started” repeated everywhere, without clarifying the action or destination.
60. Copy that contains no specific audience, use case, limitation, workflow, or real-world detail.

**Copy rule:** Write from observed product facts and the user's actual job-to-be-done. Prefer concrete nouns, verbs, workflows, examples, and honest limitations. Do not manufacture specificity either: when a fact is unknown, ask, omit it, or label the content as illustrative.

## 4. Fake credibility and artificial content (61–80)

61. “Trusted by 10,000+ users” without evidence.
62. “99.9% uptime” on a product with no visible operational history or substantiation.
63. Fake customer logos.
64. Testimonials with suspiciously generic wording.
65. Testimonials from people with no verifiable identity or company, presented as real.
66. AI-generated profile pictures used as customer testimonials.
67. Fake five-star ratings.
68. A “Loved by thousands” section with no actual reviews or evidence.
69. Fake usage statistics such as “2M+ tasks completed.”
70. Fake activity notifications such as “Someone from New York just signed up.”
71. A dashboard full of invented analytics and meaningless metrics presented as live data.
72. Fake integrations with products that are not actually supported.
73. Fake badges such as “SOC 2 Certified” or “Enterprise Ready.”
74. An “As seen in” section featuring media logos without actual coverage.
75. A fake team section with AI-generated faces presented as real employees or founders.
76. An about page that says nothing about the actual founders, team, mission, or operating reality.
77. Generic stock photography of people smiling at laptops when it does not represent the product or audience.
78. AI-generated imagery with strange hands, inconsistent details, or nonsensical text.
79. A blog containing dozens of generic SEO articles with no original insight, expertise, or editorial purpose.
80. Case studies that lack real customers, measurable outcomes, specific context, or permission to make the claims.

**Trust rule:** Never invent users, customers, people, partnerships, awards, reviews, integrations, security certifications, metrics, press coverage, performance history, or case-study outcomes. Use only claims that are supplied or verifiable. Clearly label sample content and synthetic/demo data. If genuine proof does not exist yet, explain the product, show the real workflow, and state what is still early instead of manufacturing social proof.

## 5. Interaction, accessibility, and implementation failures (81–100)

81. Every section fades in as the user scrolls.
82. Every element slides upward when it enters the viewport.
83. Random parallax effects on backgrounds.
84. A button that glows instead of providing meaningful interaction feedback.
85. Hover animations that are identical across every component.
86. A loading spinner that never leads to a useful result.
87. Buttons that look functional but do absolutely nothing.
88. Navigation links that lead to empty pages or incorrect sections.
89. No meaningful empty, loading, error, success, or disabled states.
90. A mobile version that is simply the desktop layout stacked vertically.
91. Text overflowing cards on smaller screens.
92. A dashboard that breaks at tablet widths.
93. Dropdowns and modals that feel disconnected from the rest of the interface.
94. Forms with unclear labels, generic errors, or no validation feedback.
95. Low contrast on muted text, badges, and secondary buttons.
96. Icon-only controls with no accessible labels.
97. Hover-only interactions with no keyboard equivalent.
98. Inconsistent spacing and typography between different pages.
99. A website that looks impressive in a screenshot but becomes awkward when actually used.
100. No evidence that anyone tested the website with real content, real users, or real devices.

**Experience rule:** A visible control must have a real, understandable outcome. Every relevant flow must account for loading, empty, error, success, disabled, and focus states. Design mobile and tablet as real contexts with deliberate changes to hierarchy, navigation, density, and controls—not as a desktop screenshot broken into a vertical stack. Test with the actual content shape and the available devices or viewport sizes; report honestly when user testing or device testing was not possible.

## How to apply this audit during implementation

Do not mechanically search-and-delete every item. Review the design in context and classify each possible match:

- **Remove:** the pattern is decorative, misleading, nonfunctional, repetitive, or unsupported.
- **Replace:** the intent is useful, but the execution is generic. Keep the intent and create a more specific treatment.
- **Keep with justification:** the pattern is genuinely appropriate and improves clarity, trust, accessibility, brand expression, or task completion. Keep it restrained and consistent.

Before final delivery, perform a deliberate pass across all five groups:

- **Visual identity:** Does this look like this product, or could the same screenshot belong to dozens of unrelated AI startups?
- **Page composition:** Does the section order follow the user's decision journey and real content, or a memorized landing-page recipe?
- **Language:** Could a visitor explain what the product does after reading the hero? Is every claim concrete and supportable?
- **Credibility:** Is every logo, number, quote, person, badge, integration, and customer story real or clearly marked as illustrative?
- **Usability:** Do all controls work? Are responsive layouts, accessibility, and all relevant system states accounted for?

The goal is not to make a website deliberately strange. The goal is to make it **specific, coherent, honest, useful, and recognizably designed for its actual product and users**.
